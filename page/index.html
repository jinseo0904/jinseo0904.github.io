Generating Personalized Games with Sensing Data to Support Self-Reported Data Collection

PAGE turns a participant’s own passive sensing data and self-reports into a personalized daily word puzzle. The puzzle then becomes a voluntary channel for collecting richer data.

Jin-seo Kim Ha Le Akshat Choube Varun Mishra Stephen S Intille

Northeastern University · Khoury College of Computer Sciences

📄 Paper PDF 🔗 ACM DOI 🐝 Try the demo
TL;DR

Long-term self-report studies lose participants to survey fatigue, and adding points or leaderboards doesn’t improve the data much. PAGE goes further. An LLM reads yesterday’s smartwatch self-reports and sensor data, writes a short diary of the participant’s day, and turns it into personalized clues for a word-wheel puzzle. To unlock a clue, the participant answers a quick question, which gives researchers more data. In a 2-week study (N=15), 80% preferred the puzzle week, participants gave 72 more unique contextual tags, and they answered 1,188 extra questions without payment.

The idea

Your data becomes the game.
The game collects more data.

Most gamified surveys add rewards on top of the same survey. PAGE builds the game out of the data being collected, which creates a positive feedback loop. Personalized puzzles keep participants engaged, engaged participants give richer reports, and richer reports make better puzzles.

Classic EMA

A buzz every 15 minutes: “What are you doing?” The same question over and over, and answers get shorter as the weeks go by.

Gamified EMA

Points, badges, and leaderboards are added on top. They can improve compliance, but the game has nothing to do with what you reported.

PAGE

The game is made from your own day. Yesterday’s “calling my friend on the train” becomes today’s clue: “You were ____ing your friend on your way home.” Answering a question to unlock that hint gives researchers more data.

🧩

Game module

A game people already enjoy playing regularly. The prototype uses a Spelling-Bee-style word wheel: seven letters, and every word must include the center letter.

⌚

Data collection module

Multimodal µEMA on a smartwatch (answer by tap or voice) every 15 minutes, plus passive phone and watch sensing: GPS, steps, heart rate, and more.

✨

Personalization module

A local LLM (Gemma 3, 27B) turns yesterday’s data into a diary and writes a personalized fill-in-the-blank clue for each puzzle word.

How it works

From yesterday’s data to today’s puzzle

Every night at 3 AM, PAGE turns the previous day’s data into a new puzzle. The example below follows one fictional day, based on the example in the paper’s Figure 3.

1

Collect: self-reports + passive sensing

The watch asks about posture and activity every 15 minutes, and participants answer by voice or tap. The phone and watch also passively log location, steps, heart rate, and more.

2

Synthesize: a narrative diary of your day

The data is put in time order and given to the LLM, which writes a first-person diary. The prompt tells it to keep specific details (“a latte,” not “a drink”) and not to invent anything.

3

Generate: a word wheel and its word list

A random 7-letter seed word sets the letters, and one letter is picked as the center. Every valid dictionary word is extracted. Rare, inflected, obsolete, and jargon words are then filtered out, first by word frequency and then by the LLM.

Personalize: a clue for each word, drawn from your day

The LLM links puzzle words to real moments in the diary. Words it can’t link get a “priming” clue that depends on your answer to a question, or a generic crossword-style clue as a last resort.

Design lessons

What we’d tell the next person building one

01

Self-report clues beat location clues

Participants easily connected clues to things they had said themselves. Clues from GPS often referred to places they had only passed through, which felt confusing or even unsettling. Prioritize data people recognize and feel ownership over.

02

Calibrate difficulty in both directions

Puzzles with too many words (“once I knew there were 76 words…”) made people give up before starting. Puzzles that were too easy were boring. Adjust difficulty and the strength of hints to each player.

03

Keep unlock questions from interrupting play

Eight of 15 participants didn’t mind trading answers for hints. Seven found the pop-ups disruptive. Consider fewer prompts, multiple choice only, or answering questions before the game starts.

04

Give people control over their data

Show people which data shapes their clues, and let them opt out of certain sources (such as location) at any time. The discomfort around privacy is also a chance to build better-informed consent over the course of a study.
