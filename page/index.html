Generating Personalized Games with Sensing Data to Support Self-Reported Data Collection

Personalized Adaptive Games for Engagement (PAGE) is a framework for turning a participant’s own sensing data and self-reports into personalized game content. As participants share more detail about themselves, the games become more interesting, and the games in turn become an additional channel for data collection. We test this idea with LLM-generated daily word puzzles. In the long term, we aim to use this approach to get many people to self-report voluntarily over long periods of time (without relying on financial compensation), unlocking large-scale, longitudinal health and behavioral data.

TL;DR

How do we get people to provide voluntary, rich self-reports?

Large longitudinal studies such as the NIH All of Us Research Program follow participants for years. Sensors can passively log where people go and how they move, but understanding and verifying what they were doing and why still requires repeated self-reports. Over time, participants tire of answering questions, and paying them to stay engaged doesn’t scale to studies of this size.

So we asked: can we combine self-reporting with games people already enjoy and play voluntarily, and personalize those games with the information participants provide?

We developed PAGE, a framework that builds the game out of the participant’s own data, creating a self-reinforcing feedback loop: more detailed self-reports make the game more personal and interesting, which encourages people to share more. In our case study, an LLM turns the previous day’s smartwatch µEMA self-reports and sensor data into personalized clues for a word-wheel puzzle. In a 2-week feasibility study (N=15), 80% of participants preferred the puzzle week, self-reports became richer, and participants voluntarily answered 1,188 additional questions to unlock clues.

The big picture

Studies that last for years need
self-reports that last for years

Large-scale health studies like the NIH All of Us Research Program aim to follow a million or more people for years. Sensors in phones and wearables can passively track people’s states and activities, but researchers still need self-reports to verify that data, understand what people were doing, and capture states that sensors can’t infer, such as loneliness or happiness. That usually means asking participants the same questions over and over. The problem is, people get tired of answering them.

→

Researchers can pay participants to stay engaged, but that doesn’t scale well for large longitudinal studies. Instead, we need ways to tap into participants’ intrinsic motivation, so that large numbers of people keep self-reporting voluntarily, for long periods of time.

The idea

Answer questions for science.
Enjoy your favorite games, about yourself.

The PAGE framework consists of three modules: (1) the data collection module collects individuals’ self-reports and passive sensing data, (2) the personalization module aggregates the collected data and uses it to tailor game components, and (3) the game module delivers the personalized game, which is then enjoyed by participants.

How it works

From yesterday’s data to today’s puzzle

Every night at 3 AM, PAGE turns the previous day’s data into a new puzzle. The example below follows one fictional day, based on the example in the paper’s Figure 3.

1

Collect: self-reports + passive sensing

The watch asks about posture and activity every 15 minutes, and participants answer by voice or tap. The phone and watch also passively log location, steps, heart rate, and more.

2

Synthesize: a narrative diary of your day

The data is put in time order and given to the LLM, which writes a first-person diary. The prompt tells it to keep specific details (“a latte,” not “a drink”) and not to invent anything.

3

Generate: a word wheel and its word list

A random 7-letter seed word sets the letters, and one letter is picked as the center. Every valid dictionary word is extracted. Rare, inflected, obsolete, and jargon words are then filtered out, first by word frequency and then by the LLM.

Design lessons

What we’d tell the next person building one

01

Self-report clues beat location clues

Participants easily connected clues to things they had said themselves. Clues from GPS often referred to places they had only passed through, which felt confusing or even unsettling. Prioritize data people recognize and feel ownership over.

02

Calibrate difficulty in both directions

Puzzles with too many words (“once I knew there were 76 words…”) made people give up before starting. Puzzles that were too easy were boring. Adjust difficulty and the strength of hints to each player.

03

Keep unlock questions from interrupting play

Eight of 15 participants didn’t mind trading answers for hints. Seven found the pop-ups disruptive. Consider fewer prompts, multiple choice only, or answering questions before the game starts.

04

Give people control over their data

Show people which data shapes their clues, and let them opt out of certain sources (such as location) at any time. The discomfort around privacy is also a chance to build better-informed consent over the course of a study.

Beyond word puzzles

A framework, not just a puzzle

The word puzzle is one case study. PAGE is not limited to text: the same three modules (a game, a data collection method, and a generative model that connects them) can work with other models and other kinds of content. Vision, video, and audio models could personalize games that people already enjoy in very different forms.
